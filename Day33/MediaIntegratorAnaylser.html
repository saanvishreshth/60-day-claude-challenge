Welcome

Can you spot the spin?

Every day, headlines and social posts compete for a few seconds of your attention. Some are fair. Many are engineered — with word choice, framing, and emotional triggers — to make you click, feel, and share before you think.

This is a short, hands-on lesson. You won't be quizzed on facts. Instead, you'll look at real-feeling examples, make your own call, and then see what's actually going on underneath.

You'll go through two guided challenges — Headline Detective and Emotion Detector — then get a personal Media Integrity Dashboard.
Concept · Headline Accuracy

Headlines are written to be clicked — not always to be accurate

A headline's job, in most newsrooms and nearly all social feeds, is to earn a click. That incentive can quietly pull a headline away from what the article actually says — through exaggeration, vague certainty ("proves," "stunned"), or implied urgency.

This matters because most people only ever read the headline. A 2016 study of shared links found a huge share of them were never opened at all — meaning the headline became the news for most readers.

Everyday version: you scroll past a headline, form an opinion, and move on — never realizing the article told a calmer, more complicated story.

Concept · Emotional Manipulation

Strong feelings make us stop thinking

Fear, outrage, and urgency are powerful because they short-circuit our usual "wait, is this true?" instinct. Content designed to trigger those feelings fast — often with words like "shocking," "before it's too late," or "they don't want you to know" — is optimized to be shared, not verified.

This matters because sharing usually happens in the first few seconds of feeling something, long before anyone checks a fact.

Everyday version: a post makes your stomach drop, your thumb moves to "share" — and only later (if ever) do you learn it was exaggerated or false.

    Three habits that build media integrity

    • 1Pause before you share. If a post makes you feel urgent, furious, or afraid, that's the moment to slow down — not speed up.
    • 2Read past the headline. Check whether the article's actual evidence supports the claim the headline makes.
    • 3Check who's saying it, and why. Look for the source, their evidence, and whether other credible outlets report the same thing.
